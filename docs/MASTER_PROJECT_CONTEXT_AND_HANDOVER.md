## 1. What this project is

The project identifies useful technology providers, legitimate commercial partnership routes, and existing organisations or people who can connect those technologies to relevant markets in Nigeria and beyond.

The central opportunity is not necessarily to invent or build technology. It is to find an existing solution that addresses a real problem, understand how the provider earns revenue and reaches customers, identify a trusted route to the right customer group, and determine whether the project can participate in that value chain without upfront capital.

The project therefore sits at the intersection of:

1. **Technology intelligence** — discovering and assessing companies, products, platforms, APIs and services.
2. **Commercial opportunity intelligence** — identifying affiliate, referral, reseller, channel, implementation, consulting, white-label, institutional and strategic partnership routes.
3. **Distribution-network intelligence** — mapping associations, cooperatives, federations, distributors, aggregators, professional bodies, consultants, institutions and other intermediaries that already have relevant trust and market access.
4. **Market intelligence** — understanding buyers, users, budget owners, local problems, sector structures, geography, operational constraints and adoption barriers.
5. **Opportunity qualification** — testing whether a provider, use case and network can form a real, mutually beneficial commercial arrangement.

The repository's stated mission is to identify valuable technology companies, zero-capital partnership opportunities and existing distribution networks that can connect technology solutions to markets in Nigeria and beyond.

This is a **research and opportunity-discovery project**, not currently a software-development project, an affiliate-link promotion scheme, a registered distribution company, or a confirmed partnership portfolio.

## 2. The project's core strategic idea

The project began from the observation that technology providers may have useful products but lack efficient access to relevant customers, while existing organisations and intermediaries already possess trust, relationships, local presence, professional credibility or distribution capacity.

Instead of reaching isolated customers one by one, investigate whether a legitimate network can help a provider reach a relevant group through an approved arrangement.

Potential routes include:

- An association or cooperative introducing a provider to members.
- A distributor or aggregator bundling a technology service with existing customer relationships.
- An agronomist, engineer, consultant or other specialist recommending or implementing a solution.
- A professional body or institution convening education, demonstrations or qualified introductions.
- A local partner referring qualified business opportunities to a provider that contracts, bills and supports the customer directly.
- A provider and network owner agreeing a transparent member benefit, referral fee, shared revenue or other value exchange.

The project's distribution principle is not that every large organisation is useful. Network size alone is insufficient. Relevance, active membership, trust, decision-maker access, communication channels and the ability to activate a real customer journey matter more.

This is not a pyramid or multi-level marketing model. Any compensation must be tied to genuine products, referrals, sales or services, be transparent, and be permitted by the provider and applicable rules. Recruitment-based rewards are not the objective.

## 3. Governing project principles

These principles should govern every new research branch and every continuation chat.

### 3.1 Understand first, verify second, decide third, document last

The user's practical workflow preference is explicit:

1. Explore the subject in the conversation.
2. Explain the findings in plain language.
3. Identify what is actually known and what remains unclear.
4. Verify material claims, identities, addresses, contact routes, authority and practical access.
5. Discuss what the findings mean and whether the user understands and considers them useful.
6. Decide what is worth preserving.
7. Only then update GitHub or another durable register.

Do not push unverified contacts, addresses, speculative opportunities or misunderstood material into the repository merely to appear productive. The user wants to understand practical steps before they are documented.

The user has authorised this master handover and current-state documentation. That authorisation does not mean that every future discovery should automatically be documented without regard to the research-first principle.

### 3.2 Do not repeat completed work

Project-wide non-repetition law:

> Do not repeat or go back to what has been covered; discoveries can be revisited and updated.

Continue from the latest known state. Revisit earlier work only when new evidence, a material change, a contradiction, a new use case or a necessary verification justifies it. When revisiting, state what changed and why.

### 3.3 Existing solutions before building

Do not build a competing product merely because a problem has been identified. First look for existing platforms, providers, services and startups that already address it. Investigate partnership, integration, implementation, distribution, promotion, referral, reseller or service-delivery opportunities.

Consider building only when:
- A genuine gap remains after checking existing solutions.
- There is evidence that established providers and emerging startups do not adequately address it.
- A real customer request, commercial incentive or validated need justifies development.
- The user has explicitly decided to pursue that path.

### 3.4 Zero-upfront-capital constraint

The initial constraint is to find opportunities that do not require the user to finance:
- Joining or registration fees.
- Compulsory product purchases or deposits.
- Inventory or stockholding.
- Prepaid licences, hectares or credits.
- Equipment.
- Mandatory paid training or certification.
- Material minimum sales commitments or other financial exposure.

A programme advertised as free may still impose substantial unpaid labour, support, technical or operating costs. Record those separately. Do not pay to unlock a promised commission. Do not assume a route is zero-capital until the full terms are checked.

### 3.5 Never depend on one technology provider

Farmonaut was the initial case study, not the permanent centre of the project. For each customer problem, identify direct competitors, substitutes, adjacent and complementary technologies. Compare technical fit separately from partnership accessibility. Keep multiple options open where credible alternatives exist.

### 3.6 Evidence over claims

A website, directory, social post, marketing claim, event listing or historical report is not the same as current independent confirmation. Distinguish provider claims, official documents, independent evidence, user-supplied material, hypotheses and unknowns.

### 3.7 Network activation over nominal reach

Do not treat an organisation as a partner because it exists, has a website, lists a large membership or has a prominent leader. Track the stages from identification through verified contact, introduction, interest, authority, agreed mechanism, member access, qualified leads and actual adoption.

### 3.8 No premature outreach or execution

Do not email, call, message, submit forms, register, pay, share personal information, arrange meetings or contact organisations on the user's behalf unless the user explicitly authorises that action. Researching a public contact channel is not permission to use it.

### 3.9 Keep business tracks separate

Different products, buyers, sectors, use cases, geographies and partnership terms must not be blended into one generic opportunity. In particular, Farmonaut agriculture and mining are separate tracks. Agriculture itself should not be reduced to one crop or one local community unless the user explicitly chooses a bounded pilot question.

### 3.10 Preserve negative and unresolved findings

Record why a route is deferred or rejected so the project does not cycle back to it without new evidence. An unknown is not a positive finding.

## 5. How a successful opportunity should work

A viable arrangement should create clear value for four parties:

1. **Technology provider:** reaches a relevant customer segment, qualified leads or a new market.
2. **Network owner/intermediary:** receives a permitted benefit, useful service for members, new capability or transparent commercial value.
3. **End customer/member:** gets a relevant solution to a real problem, with clear pricing, support and expectations.
4. **Project operator:** earns an agreed referral fee, commission, margin or service income for coordinating legitimate access and value delivery.

The precise role may be affiliate, referrer, reseller, channel partner, consultant, implementation partner, integrator, local service partner or institutional connector. Do not assume the most complex role is necessary. Prefer the lowest-friction legitimate route that delivers value.

## 6. Standard research workflow

Use this sequence for each provider/use-case/network combination.

### Stage A — Define the problem and customer
- What real problem is being addressed?
- Who experiences it?
- Who uses the product?
- Who owns the budget and can approve purchase?
- What does the customer do today?
- What evidence shows the problem is significant?
- What local conditions affect adoption?

### Stage B — Understand the technology
- What does the product actually do?
- Which features are relevant to the defined use case?
- What are the product's limits and dependencies?
- What evidence supports its claimed performance?
- What local, language, regulatory, infrastructure or integration requirements apply?
- What alternatives and complements exist?

### Stage C — Qualify the provider's commercial route
- Is there an affiliate, referral, reseller, channel, institutional or service-partner programme?
- Can an individual based in Nigeria participate?
- Is company registration required?
- Are there fees, purchases, inventory, equipment, certification or sales targets?
- What triggers compensation?
- What are the commission/margin, recurrence, attribution and payout rules?
- Can the provider contract, bill, onboard and support the customer directly?
- Are association-level referrals, co-branding or shared revenue permitted?

### Stage D — Map the distribution ecosystem
- Identify relevant organisations, branches, members and intermediaries.
- Verify legal/operational identity and current activity.
- Identify current authorised decision-makers and contact routes.
- Determine whether the network reaches the actual buyer/user group.
- Establish active membership, communication, convening and introduction capacity.
- Check existing provider relationships, conflicts and incentives.

### Stage E — Design the value exchange
Do not ask a network merely to share a link. Define:
- Member/customer problem.
- Solution and expected benefit.
- Provider's role.
- Network's role.
- Project's role.
- Who pays and who contracts.
- Transparent benefit or compensation.
- Support, data, privacy, branding and liability responsibilities.

### Stage F — Validate and decide
Use explicit decision gates. Do not advance to pilot, outreach or execution while critical technical, commercial, network or safety requirements remain unknown.

## 7. Evidence and status language

Use the repository's evidence hierarchy:
1. Current written confirmation from an authorised provider/organisation representative.
2. Current official terms, policies, registers or formal documents.
3. Official organisation pages and dated announcements.
4. Credible independent reporting and sector sources.
5. Third-party directories and event listings.
6. Social posts and uncorroborated comments.

For each material claim, preserve source, source type, date, access date, excerpt/summary, whether self-reported or independently corroborated, confidence and next action.

Use these statuses consistently:
- **Unverified lead:** identified; material facts not checked.
- **Evidence found:** a source supports the claim but it is not fully confirmed.
- **Provider-confirmed:** the provider has explicitly confirmed the relevant point.
- **Network-confirmed:** the organisation or authorised representative has confirmed the relevant point.
- **Qualified:** essential market, technical, commercial and access requirements are sufficiently met.
- **Conditional:** plausible, but material questions remain.
- **Deferred:** not actionable under present constraints.
- **Rejected:** fails a required criterion or evidence contradicts the proposition.

For networks, separately record the activation stage: identified, identity checked, decision-maker identified, contact verified, introduction obtained, interest expressed, authority/terms discussed, distribution mechanism agreed, members reached, qualified leads/demonstrations generated, adoption/sales evidenced.

## 8. Partnership models under consideration

| Model | Typical role | Essential checks |
|---|---|---|
| Affiliate | Trackable link/code and commission | Eligibility, tracking, payout, permitted promotion |
| Referral partner | Qualified lead or introduction | Lead registration, acceptance, fee trigger, payment |
| Reseller | Sells subscriptions/services at margin | Authorisation, pricing, purchase commitments, support |
| Distributor/channel | Serves a defined market/territory | Territory, targets, training, exclusivity, minimums |
| Strategic/institutional | Joint programme with an institution | Authority, responsibilities, benefits, branding, data |
| Consultant/integrator | Recommends or implements as a service | Competence, certification, liability, support |
| White-label/co-branded | Joint or rebranded service | Branding rights, product ownership, customer support, economics |

Preferred initial route: no-fee individual referral where genuinely permitted, with the provider handling contracting, billing, onboarding and technical support. Institutional/network-level distribution should be explored only when the provider permits it and the network's role and value are clear.

### 10.5 Nigeria agriculture provider/use-case comparison

The repository compares Farmonaut, EOSDA, Cropin and OneSoil against four proposed use cases:
1. Cooperative or association field oversight.
2. Agronomist-led scouting and service delivery.
3. Aggregator/outgrower production and supply visibility.
4. Lender, insurer or development-programme portfolio monitoring.

A proposed service hypothesis is a **group crop-monitoring and field-prioritisation service**: use satellite indicators to help an organisation identify fields that may need attention, then have agronomists or extension workers inspect them. This is a hypothesis, not proven Nigerian product-market fit. Satellite indicators do not diagnose every cause of crop stress and should not be used as a substitute for field inspection and local agronomic judgement.

The comparison's preliminary provider roles:
- Farmonaut: visible low-friction affiliate route; group monitoring workflow and Nigeria eligibility still need validation.
- Cropin: strongest apparent enterprise/institutional scope for aggregators, processors, lenders, insurers and programmes; individual partner access and economics unknown.
- OneSoil: relevant to agronomist-led scouting and service providers; dealer conditions unknown.
- EOSDA: technical alternative and Nigeria white-label precedent, but published reseller pre-purchase requirements conflict with the zero-capital constraint unless an exception/customer-funded model exists.

These are research priorities, not a selected technical winner.
