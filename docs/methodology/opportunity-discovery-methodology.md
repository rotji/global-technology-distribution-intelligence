# Opportunity Discovery Methodology

## Purpose

Provide a repeatable, evidence-led method for discovering and comparing opportunities across locations, sectors, communities, value chains and economic environments.

This is the parent discovery method for the project. Specialist methods—such as technology-provider assessment, network intelligence, evidence verification and distribution strategy—are applied when the discovery question calls for them. They do not define the entire mission or dictate where every investigation must begin.

## Governing sequence

Understand the environment before prescribing a solution. Explore broadly before committing narrowly. Investigate existing solutions before considering a build. Deepen research when evidence, urgency or a material decision justifies it.

## Stage 1 — Define the discovery boundary

Record:
- The broad question or reason for exploring.
- Geographic boundary and relevant administrative levels.
- Sectors, economic activities or value chains that may be relevant.
- Time period and date of the research snapshot.
- Known constraints, including safety, access, budget and zero-capital requirements.
- What is already documented and should not be repeated.

A boundary makes research manageable; it must not become an unsupported assumption about where the best opportunity lies.

## Stage 2 — Explore the environment broadly

Depending on the place and research question, examine:
- Communities, settlements and administrative areas.
- Economic activities, livelihoods, sectors and value chains.
- Producers, traders, service businesses, processors, buyers and consumers.
- Formal institutions, public agencies, programmes and private organisations.
- Informal groups, local leadership, community structures and practical coordination arrangements.
- Infrastructure, transport, energy, communications, storage, water and logistics.
- Skills, labour, knowledge, equipment, natural and other locally available resources.
- Existing technologies, products, services and operating practices.
- Market relationships, information flows, access barriers and dependencies.
- Problems, constraints, unmet needs and adaptations already used by participants.

Do not assume that every location has formal associations or cooperatives. Do not force discoveries into a preselected sector, crop, organisation type or technology category.

## Stage 3 — Capture discoveries without premature ranking

Create dated records for material findings. For each discovery, capture as applicable:
- Discovery ID and concise title.
- Location, sector, activity and relevant actors.
- What was observed or reported.
- Who experiences the issue or provides the capability.
- Existing practice or solution.
- Potential implications or opportunity hypothesis.
- Source, date, evidence type and confidence.
- What remains unknown.
- Related discoveries and records.
- Suggested verification question, not an assumed next action.

Keep facts, reported claims, inference and hypotheses visibly distinct. An empty or unresearched area in a register is not evidence that no activity or opportunity exists there.

## Stage 4 — Compare coverage and discoveries

Compare across relevant locations, sectors, actors and activities before selecting a focus. Consider:
- Whether research coverage is balanced enough for the question.
- Scale and recurrence of the reported problem or capability.
- Who is affected and how severely.
- Whether there is an identifiable user, buyer, budget owner or decision authority.
- Existing solutions and how well they work in practice.
- Local capacity, resources, infrastructure and implementation conditions.
- Access, trust, safety, regulation and operational barriers.
- Evidence quality, recency and remaining uncertainty.
- Whether the opportunity can be explored within the user's constraints.

Do not convert missing information into a positive score. Record why a candidate is prioritised, deferred or excluded.

## Stage 5 — Understand the problem and value

For a promising discovery, establish:
1. What exactly happens, and in what context?
2. Who experiences the problem or needs the capability?
3. How is it currently handled?
4. What are the consequences of the current approach?
5. How frequent, material and urgent is the issue?
6. Who benefits from a better outcome?
7. Who would use, approve, procure or pay for a solution?
8. What evidence would demonstrate that the proposed improvement matters?

A visible difficulty does not automatically imply willingness or ability to pay. Distinguish social value, operational value, public-programme priorities and commercial demand.

## Stage 6 — Map existing solutions before proposing a new one

Investigate:
- Existing local providers and service operators.
- Established national and international platforms.
- Emerging startups and specialist suppliers.
- Manual, informal and low-technology alternatives.
- Direct competitors, functional substitutes, adjacent and complementary solutions.
- Existing institutional programmes or infrastructure that may already address the need.

Assess real capability, availability, local fit, price, access, implementation burden, reliability and limitations. A provider's marketing claims are not independent proof of performance.

Prefer using, partnering with, integrating, implementing, referring or distributing an existing solution where it can meet the need. Consider building only after a genuine gap remains, a concrete customer request or incentive exists, feasibility is assessed and the user approves moving beyond research.

## Stage 7 — Identify routes to value

Depending on the discovery, consider:
- Existing provider referral or affiliate arrangements.
- Reseller, channel, distribution or institutional partnerships.
- Local implementation, onboarding, training or support.
- Integration and managed-service opportunities.
- Market access, procurement or supplier relationships.
- Existing community, professional or commercial networks.
- Public programmes, research institutions or development initiatives.
- A genuine unmet service gap that warrants further validation.

Use the [Network Intelligence Methodology](network-intelligence-methodology.md) only where mapping a network will answer a real question. Use the [Technology Company Assessment Methodology](technology-company-assessment.md) when assessing a provider.

## Stage 8 — Verify and decide

Use the [Evidence and Verification](evidence-and-verification.md) rules. Where relevant, assess:
- Problem and customer evidence.
- Technical suitability and demonstrated performance.
- Existing alternatives.
- Provider or organisation identity and authority.
- Commercial terms, eligibility and payout.
- Zero-upfront-capital compatibility and hidden costs.
- Local implementation and support capacity.
- Safety, legal, regulatory, privacy and reputational risks.
- Clear responsibilities and value exchange.

Possible outcomes include:
- **Continue research:** meaningful questions remain and further evidence is justified.
- **Prioritise validation:** a bounded test could materially change a decision.
- **Conditional:** promising, but material requirements remain unconfirmed.
- **Defer:** not actionable under current constraints or timing.
- **Reject:** evidence shows the proposition is unsuitable or fails a required criterion.
- **Monitor:** no immediate action, but a defined future change could make it relevant.

Record the rationale, evidence, confidence, unresolved questions and what would change the decision.

## Stage 9 — Deep dive or pilot only when justified

A deep dive may be justified by:
- A material unresolved question that blocks a decision.
- Credible evidence of a significant opportunity.
- An urgent problem or time-sensitive development.
- A specific customer request, institutional requirement or provider invitation.
- A need to validate safety, technical feasibility or commercial assumptions.

Before proceeding, state the question, decision it could change, evidence required, scope, constraints and stopping conditions. A pilot is not authorised merely because it is described in a research record; obtain the user's explicit approval before external engagement, commitments or execution.

## Breadth-first exceptions

Broad-first exploration is a default, not a rigid rule. An unusually promising or urgent discovery may justify an early deep dive. Record why it takes priority, what broader comparison remains incomplete and how the risk of premature concentration will be managed.

## Continuity and non-duplication

- Read relevant existing records before starting.
- Do not repeat completed work without a reason under the research principles.
- Add new dated evidence to the appropriate source record and preserve earlier findings and dates.
- Link related discoveries rather than copying the same content into multiple documents.
- Update decision records when evidence changes a decision.
- The README is an index, not a live status database; the changelog records repository edits, not the current truth of external conditions.
