# Network Intelligence Methodology

## Purpose and place within the project

This methodology identifies, profiles and evaluates organisations or intermediaries that may provide legitimate access, trust, expertise, coordination, implementation capacity or routes to market.

Network intelligence is a specialised method within the wider opportunity-discovery process. It should normally be used after an initial problem, market, service or opportunity has been identified and there is a reason to investigate whether an existing network can help. Do not assume that every discovery requires an association, cooperative or formal intermediary.

A network may be relevant as a customer, buyer, beneficiary, service provider, implementation partner, source of expertise, regulator, funder, connector or distribution route. Record its actual role rather than automatically classifying it as a channel.

## When to map a network

Map a network when evidence suggests that:
- Relevant people or businesses are organised through it.
- It has a credible relationship with the target users, buyers or decision-makers.
- It controls or enables access, convening, information, trust, technical support or implementation.
- It can help validate a problem, coordinate a service or facilitate adoption.
- Its role could improve the feasibility or value of a specific opportunity.

Do not prioritise an organisation solely because it is prominent, has a large stated membership or is easy to find online.

## Network categories

Possible categories include:
- Associations, federations and professional bodies.
- Cooperatives, producer groups and community organisations.
- Distributors, aggregators, wholesalers and supply-chain intermediaries.
- Industry connectors, consultants and specialist advisers.
- Incubators, accelerators and business-support organisations.
- Marketing agencies, audience networks and specialist media.
- Financial institutions, insurers, lenders and payment networks.
- Educational, research, extension and training institutions.
- Public agencies, regulators and development programmes.
- Informal groups and local coordination structures.

These are discovery categories, not a requirement that every community or sector must contain a formal organisation of each type. Informal and practical relationships may be more relevant than registered structures.

## Network record

Capture, where relevant:
- Unique network ID and organisation or group name.
- Actual role in the opportunity: buyer, user, beneficiary, provider, connector, regulator, funder, implementer or distribution intermediary.
- Type, sector and relevant activities.
- Official website and public contact channels, if available.
- Legal or operational identity and legitimacy evidence.
- Geography, local presence and actual operating footprint.
- Stated membership or reach, with source and date.
- Evidence of active members, participants, customers or recent activity.
- Leadership, decision authority and relevant decision-makers.
- Communication, coordination and convening channels.
- Relevant member, customer or beneficiary segments.
- Existing commercial, institutional or technology relationships.
- Ability to introduce, convene, educate, validate, implement, refer or distribute.
- Resources, capabilities and constraints.
- Incentives, costs, conflicts and potential risks.
- Evidence confidence, unresolved questions and next verification action.

Do not assume that an organisation's national mandate translates into active local reach. Verify the relevant branch, chapter, community or operating unit.

## Network activation ladder

Distinguish the stages actually evidenced:
1. Organisation or group identified.
2. Identity and activity checked.
3. Relevant role in the opportunity established.
4. Decision-maker or authorised representative identified.
5. Contact route verified.
6. Introduction obtained.
7. Interest expressed.
8. Authority, responsibilities and terms discussed.
9. A defined role or arrangement agreed.
10. Relevant users, members or customers reached.
11. Demonstration, service or implementation delivered.
12. Qualified leads, adoption, sales or other outcomes evidenced.

Do not describe an organisation as a distribution partner merely because it has been identified, contacted or has expressed interest. Use the stage that the evidence supports.

## Comparison and scorecard

Use the project's weighted scorecard in [Distribution Network Strategy](../strategy/distribution-network-strategy.md) only when comparing candidates for a defined role. Confirm that the criteria are relevant to that role; a regulator, buyer, technical institution and distribution intermediary should not automatically be scored as though they perform the same function.

Score only with evidence. Mark unknown dimensions as unknown rather than assigning optimistic points. Explain material differences and do not use a numeric score as a substitute for judgment.

## Relationship mapping

Map direct routes and potential connectors. A connector's relevance depends on whether it can credibly connect the project to the specific user, buyer, decision-maker or capability needed—not merely whether it is influential in general.

Record the nature of the relationship and whether it is evidenced, reported, inferred or only a hypothesis. Do not infer endorsement, authority, access to private member data or permission to act.

## Safeguards

- Do not contact an organisation or representative without the user's explicit authorisation.
- Do not use private member lists or personal contact data without proper authority and consent.
- Do not imply endorsement or partnership without permission.
- Do not promise commissions, benefits or shared revenue before the relevant parties approve the arrangement.
- Do not mistake a formal registration, public profile or historic announcement for current activity.
- Record negative findings and inactive, inaccessible or unsuitable networks rather than silently dropping them.
