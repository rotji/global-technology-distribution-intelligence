# Pilot Market Selection: Plateau State Maize Growers

**Research snapshot:** 2026-11-09  
**Status:** Preliminary market-selection decision. Evidence supports a research focus, not a confirmed customer, network partner or commercial opportunity.  
**Scope selected:** Maize growers in Plateau State, with farmer cooperatives and organised producer groups as the initial distribution hypothesis.

> **Scope clarification added 2026-11-11:** This is a historical, bounded research hypothesis from the initial Plateau agriculture investigation. It is not a permanent project strategy, a decision to prioritise maize across Plateau, or a mandate to select a cooperative or pilot location. The parent project now uses broad-first discovery across Plateau's 17 LGAs and across economic activities, communities, institutions and services. Keep this record as evidence of the earlier maize hypothesis; compare it with other discoveries before deciding whether to revisit it.

## 1. Historical selection rationale

Use **maize growers in Plateau State** as the first bounded market to investigate.

This is a research focus, not an irreversible commitment. It is selected because several evidence streams converge:

1. Plateau is identified among Nigeria's major maize-producing states in national agricultural material.
2. A 2024 Plateau farmer-engagement report documents challenges affecting maize, soybean and rice farmers, including late or poor-quality inputs, limited extension support, climate and pest pressures.
3. A 2025 study of 210 smallholder maize farmers in Plateau reports that 58% belonged to cooperatives, suggesting that cooperative structures are a relevant route to investigate.
4. Plateau has existing farmer-organisation leads already recorded in this project, including AFAN and local cooperative structures.
5. The project can test a local distribution hypothesis without immediately expanding into several states or crop value chains.

**Why not start with rice?** Rice is also relevant in Plateau and nationally. However, the evidence assembled for this pass is more specific to Plateau maize growers and cooperative membership. Rice remains a second crop track, not rejected.

### 2.2 Existing cooperative behaviour
A 2025 study of 210 smallholder maize farmers in Plateau reports:
- Average farm size of approximately 3 hectares.
- 58% of respondents belonged to cooperative societies.
- Low frequency of sustainable agricultural practice adoption.
- Reported constraints included limited awareness and technical knowledge, credit constraints, poor infrastructure, limited government support and unpredictable weather.

A separate 2025 study of 210 maize farmers across three Plateau agricultural zones reports 58% cooperative membership and low climate-change awareness, with resource constraints affecting adaptation.

These are study-sample findings, not a census of all Plateau maize farmers. The sample does not prove that any particular cooperative is active or accessible to this project.

### 2.3 Documented farmer pain points
The AFAP 2024 Plateau report records farmer concerns about:
- Late arrival of subsidised inputs.
- Limited information and insufficient technical knowledge among some agrochemical vendors.
- Poor seed quality and counterfeit or poor-quality inputs.
- High input costs and machinery shortages.
- Limited extension services.
- Rainfall variability, pests, insecurity and post-harvest constraints.

The report's engagement included farmers, officials and agricultural stakeholders, and describes visits and discussions in August 2024. These findings are useful context but should not be treated as a current 2026 needs assessment.

## 3. The specific problem hypothesis

Do not begin with the broad claim that farmers need satellite monitoring. Start with a problem that can be tested:

> A cooperative or farmer-support organisation serving dispersed maize growers may need a practical way to identify fields showing unusual crop-development changes, prioritise extension visits and maintain a clearer overview of member fields during the growing season.

Potential workflow:
1. Record consenting member farms and field boundaries.
2. Record crop, planting date and basic field information.
3. Monitor fields with a suitable satellite crop-monitoring provider.
4. Generate a periodic exception list of fields or zones showing unusual vegetation or moisture signals.
5. Route those exceptions to an agronomist or extension worker for physical inspection.
6. Record what was found and whether follow-up was useful.

This is a **triage and field-prioritisation** proposition. Satellite indicators do not independently diagnose a pest, nutrient deficiency, disease or water problem. Local agronomic assessment is required before recommending action.

## 4. Who might benefit and who might pay?

| Stakeholder | Potential value | What must be validated |
|---|---|---|
| Farmer cooperative | Shared visibility of member fields and prioritised support | Whether leadership sees this as a real need and can coordinate members |
| Aggregator / outgrower operator | Better awareness of crop progress and potential supply disruptions | Whether the operator has a grower registry and a budget for monitoring |
| Agronomist / extension provider | Prioritise field visits using satellite signals as one input | Whether alerts are actionable and save time |
| Processor / buyer | Better visibility of production clusters and potential sourcing risks | Whether monitoring can improve procurement decisions enough to justify cost |
| Individual farmer | More information about their own field | Affordability and ability to act on information |

The most plausible first payer may be an organisation already coordinating many farms, rather than an individual smallholder. This is a hypothesis to test, not an established conclusion.

Do not select Mangu as a pilot location solely because of its maize significance. Any LGA or community selection must include current safety, accessibility and local-partner validation.

### AFAN leadership and governance caution

BusinessDay reported on 3 March 2026 that John Wuyep was chairman of the AFAN Plateau State chapter and described the effects of insecurity on farmers in areas including Bokkos. This is useful recent evidence of a publicly identified chapter representative, but it does not by itself establish current authority to approve a partnership or convene maize farmers.

AFAN has also experienced nationally reported leadership disputes. BusinessDay reported calls for unity in August 2025, while reporting in 2026 continued to describe harmonisation efforts. Before relying on AFAN as an introduction route, verify the current national and Plateau chapter leadership, the authority chain, and which recognised structure can validly represent the relevant members.

**Assessment:** AFAN Plateau is a relevant lead with a current public reference, but its governance and authority must be confirmed before it is treated as a distribution partner.

No provider has been selected as the technical winner. Product suitability must be demonstrated for the specific crop, geography, farm sizes, season and operating workflow.

## 7. Minimum evidence required before a pilot

### Market and network
- One real, active cooperative or producer organisation.
- An authorised contact who can explain its structure and approve member engagement.
- Evidence of active maize growers and their locations.
- Approximate number of growers and plots willing to participate.
- Crop calendar, planting dates and basic farm-boundary availability.
- Existing extension or agronomy support and how field visits are arranged.

### Technical
- Provider confirms maize support and explains what the model actually measures.
- Provider explains revisit frequency and cloud/rainy-season limitations.
- Multi-field account and consolidated reporting are demonstrated.
- Sample field boundaries can be tested with consent.
- An agronomist can validate selected alerts against field observations.

### Commercial
- Provider confirms Nigeria eligibility and the exact partner model.
- No fee, deposit, compulsory purchase, prepaid hectares, inventory or paid certification.
- Provider confirms who contracts, bills, onboards and supports the customer.
- Written commission/margin, payout, attribution and lead-protection terms.
- Permission for an association or cooperative to participate in a commercial arrangement or receive an agreed benefit.

## 8. Decision gates

1. **Market validation:** confirm an active maize-growing organisation and a real monitoring/extension problem.
2. **Technical validation:** demonstrate that at least one provider's field signals are useful in the selected local context.
3. **Commercial validation:** confirm a genuinely zero-capital route and Nigeria payout eligibility.
4. **Network validation:** confirm the organisation can mobilise consenting members and coordinate field follow-up.
5. **Pilot design:** only after the preceding gates, define a small, bounded, consent-based demonstration.

If any essential gate fails, defer or change the use case. Do not force a provider or network into the model.
