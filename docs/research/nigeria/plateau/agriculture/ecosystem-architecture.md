# Plateau Agriculture Ecosystem Architecture

## Purpose and status

This document defines the **scope and organising architecture** for understanding the agricultural ecosystem across Plateau State. It is a research map: it identifies the domains, systems, actors, relationships and questions that may matter.

It is **not** a directory of organisations, a list of confirmed programmes, a statement that every listed capability exists in Plateau, or a record of verified agricultural conditions. The topics below are investigation categories, not factual claims about local presence or activity.

The architecture is deliberately broader than farming and commodity value chains. Agriculture is influenced by science, law, public policy, standards, information, infrastructure, finance, education, health, environment and developments in adjacent sectors. Seemingly peripheral systems should not be excluded when they can materially affect agricultural decisions, costs, risks, productivity, market access or livelihoods.

## Geographic frame

Use Plateau State's 17 Local Government Areas as the initial geographic frame, while recognising that ecological zones, watersheds, catchments, markets, transport corridors, customary territories and value-chain footprints may cross LGA boundaries.

Community-level research should not assume that a community name, ward, polling unit, traditional area, watershed or programme boundary is interchangeable with an official administrative boundary. Record the type of geography being discussed and verify boundary claims before making them.

## Ecosystem domains

The domains are a discovery checklist. They are not a ranking, and not every domain will be equally relevant to every research question.

### 1. Land, ecology and natural-resource systems

Topics to examine:
- Land access, tenure, customary arrangements, land use and competing uses.
- Soil types, soil health, fertility, erosion and land degradation.
- Water resources, watersheds, irrigation, drainage and groundwater.
- Climate, rainfall, temperature, microclimates, drought, floods and extreme weather.
- Biodiversity, pollinators, forests, rangelands and ecosystem services.
- Environmental change, pollution, waste, restoration and climate adaptation.
- Interactions with mining, urban expansion, infrastructure and other land uses.

### 2. Agricultural production and biological systems

Topics to examine:
- Field crops, horticulture, roots and tubers, grains, legumes, oilseeds and other crop systems.
- Livestock, poultry, dairy, animal feed, animal health and veterinary services.
- Fisheries, aquaculture and other relevant production systems.
- Seed systems, planting materials, breeding, genetics and varieties.
- Fertiliser, soil amendments, crop protection, pesticides and integrated pest management.
- Farm operations, mechanisation, tools, irrigation, energy and water management.
- Production models, farm sizes, seasonal calendars, labour and productivity.
- Plant pests, animal diseases, biosecurity and surveillance.

### 3. Post-harvest, processing and value chains

Topics to examine:
- Harvesting, handling, drying, sorting, grading and packaging.
- Storage, cold chains, warehouses, silos and loss prevention.
- Aggregation, collection centres, processors and food manufacturing.
- Transport, logistics, wholesale, retail, exports and cross-border trade.
- Inputs and equipment supply chains, spare parts, repairs and maintenance.
- By-products, waste streams, circular use and bio-based products.
- Commodity-specific and multi-commodity value-chain relationships.

### 4. People, livelihoods and organisational structures

Topics to examine:
- Individual farmers, pastoralists, fishers, processors, traders and service businesses.
- Household livelihoods, women, youth and other relevant participant groups.
- Informal working groups, producer groups, cooperatives and community organisations.
- Traditional institutions, community leadership and local coordination systems.
- Farmer associations, commodity associations, unions and professional bodies.
- Extension personnel, agronomists, veterinarians, researchers and independent advisers.
- Labour availability, skills, migration, seasonal work and labour conditions.

Do not assume that a formal cooperative or registered association exists, is active, represents a whole community or is the best route to reach participants.

### 5. Research, science, laboratories and innovation

This domain is explicitly part of the agricultural picture, not an optional technical add-on.

Topics to examine:
- Universities, faculties, departments, research institutes and specialist centres.
- Agricultural research stations, experimental farms, demonstration plots and field trials.
- Soil-testing, water-testing, seed-testing and plant/animal diagnostic laboratories.
- Veterinary, plant-health, pest, disease, food-safety and post-harvest laboratories.
- Biotechnology, breeding, genomics, tissue culture and other relevant scientific capabilities.
- Research projects, published studies, theses, datasets, scientific networks and research partnerships.
- Technology transfer, intellectual property, patents, licensing and commercialisation.
- Research funding, grants, donor programmes and public/private R&D investment.
- The path from research findings to field validation, extension, adoption and commercial use.

A laboratory, research centre or university appearing in a source does not by itself establish that it currently offers a service, has capacity, is accessible, or is relevant to a particular community. Verify the specific capability and its operational status before recording it as a factual local resource.

### 6. Law, public policy, regulation and governance

This domain includes formal rules and the institutions that make, interpret, enforce or administer them.

Topics to examine:
- Agricultural, food-system, rural-development and industrial policies.
- Land tenure, land administration, customary rights and land-use planning.
- Water rights, irrigation rules and environmental protection.
- Seed, fertiliser, pesticide and agrochemical regulation.
- Plant health, animal health, veterinary practice and biosecurity rules.
- Food safety, public health, product standards, packaging and labelling.
- Processing, storage, transport, market, import/export and trade requirements.
- Taxation, customs, public procurement, subsidies, grants and public expenditure.
- Cooperative, association, company, labour and occupational-safety rules.
- Federal, state and local responsibilities; regulators, ministries, agencies and enforcement arrangements.
- Policy changes, court decisions, implementation gaps and differences between written rules and practice.

Distinguish a law or policy being announced, proposed, enacted, commenced, implemented, enforced or amended. These are not interchangeable statuses. Record the exact legal instrument and authoritative source when making a legal or regulatory claim.

### 7. Standards, testing, quality and certification systems

Topics to examine:
- Product, process, input and equipment standards.
- Testing, inspection, conformity assessment and laboratory accreditation.
- Seed, soil, water, animal-health and food-quality certification.
- Traceability, grading, origin, sustainability and export requirements.
- Quality assurance, certification costs, audit processes and market acceptance.
- Public regulators, standards bodies, private certifiers and buyer-specific requirements.

Do not infer that a product is certified, a laboratory accredited or a provider authorised merely because it offers testing or certification-related services.

### 8. Knowledge, data and information networks

Information systems can influence agriculture even when they do not appear to be agricultural organisations.

Topics to examine:
- Public agricultural statistics, censuses, administrative records and open datasets.
- Market prices, buyer demand, commodity intelligence and trade information.
- Weather forecasts, seasonal outlooks, climate data and early-warning systems.
- Pest, disease, livestock and plant-health alerts and surveillance networks.
- Extension communication, farmer knowledge exchange and demonstration networks.
- Universities, journals, libraries, repositories, scientific publications and technical reports.
- Radio, newspapers, television, community media, agricultural programmes and digital media.
- Telecoms, mobile networks, internet access, digital identity, payment and data platforms.
- Mapping, remote sensing, satellite imagery, GIS and geospatial intelligence.
- Professional networks, conferences, workshops, online communities and informal knowledge brokers.
- Misinformation, information asymmetry, language, access, trust and data quality.

Investigate who produces information, who receives it, how frequently it is updated, who can access it, how reliable it is and whether it changes a real decision. A channel's existence does not prove reach, trust, adoption or usefulness.

### 9. Finance, investment, insurance and risk

Topics to examine:
- Agricultural credit, microfinance, commercial banks and development finance.
- Grants, public funds, donor finance, revolving funds and community finance.
- Input credit, buyer advances, contract finance and value-chain finance.
- Savings groups, cooperatives and informal finance.
- Crop, livestock, asset, health and weather-related insurance.
- Investment, guarantees, blended finance, venture funding and impact capital.
- Interest, fees, collateral, repayment, eligibility, risk allocation and hidden costs.
- Financial literacy, payment infrastructure and access to financial services.

Distinguish announced funding, approved funding, disbursed funding, accessible finance and finance actually used. Never assume that a funding programme is a grant or that a stated allocation has reached intended beneficiaries.

### 10. Physical infrastructure, utilities and logistics

Topics to examine:
- Roads, bridges, transport routes, markets and collection points.
- Electricity, fuel, renewable energy and energy reliability.
- Water supply, irrigation, drainage and sanitation.
- Warehouses, silos, cold rooms, processing facilities and laboratories.
- Telecoms, internet connectivity and digital infrastructure.
- Equipment, machinery, workshops, fabrication, repair and spare-parts networks.
- Public facilities, private facilities and community-managed infrastructure.
- Infrastructure ownership, operating condition, access, capacity, tariffs and maintenance.

A facility's construction or announcement does not prove completion, operational status, available capacity or affordable access.

### 11. Technology, products and service-provider landscape

Topics to examine:
- Agricultural technology, farm management and advisory systems.
- Remote sensing, GIS, drones, sensors and precision agriculture.
- Mechanisation, irrigation, energy, storage and processing technologies.
- Digital marketplaces, payments, logistics and traceability.
- Input suppliers, equipment vendors, repairers, laboratories and technical services.
- Local inventors, fabricators, startups, established companies and informal solution providers.
- Alternatives, substitutes, manual practices, open-source tools and low-cost adaptations.
- Pricing, local support, implementation, interoperability, ownership and maintenance.

Compare existing solutions and local practices before recommending a new build. A technology's availability elsewhere does not prove local suitability, demand or adoption.

### 12. Markets, demand, trade and external influences

Topics to examine:
- Household consumption, institutional buyers, processors, retailers and wholesalers.
- Public procurement, school feeding, hospitals and other institutional demand where relevant.
- Domestic and regional trade, imports, exports and border processes.
- Commodity prices, exchange rates, inflation, fuel and input-price movements.
- Buyer specifications, contract terms, payment reliability and market concentration.
- Food security, nutrition, population change, urbanisation and changing consumption.
- National and international policy, trade agreements, geopolitical disruptions and supply-chain shocks.
- Competition from other producing regions and substitute products.

Do not treat production volume as proof of a profitable market. Examine demand, quality requirements, logistics, pricing, payment and the distribution of value.

### 13. Adjacent systems and weak signals

Scan beyond conventional agriculture categories when developments could materially affect the sector. Examples include:
- Mining, construction, urban development and competing land or water demands.
- Public health, nutrition, sanitation and zoonotic disease.
- Education, vocational training, research policy and workforce development.
- Energy policy, fuel supply, transport policy and telecommunications.
- Security, conflict, displacement, migration and access constraints.
- Waste management, environmental enforcement and climate programmes.
- New scientific discoveries, standards, regulations, infrastructure plans or information systems.

These are prompts for investigation, not assertions that a particular issue is present in a Plateau community. Include an adjacent topic in a research record only when its relevance and evidence are clear.

## Relationships and influence pathways

Do not study the domains as isolated lists. Where evidence permits, trace how one system affects another. Examples of questions include:
- How do land and water rules affect production choices and investment?
- How do research institutions and laboratories connect to extension, farmers and commercial users?
- How do standards, testing and certification affect access to processors, retailers or export markets?
- How do weather information and disease alerts reach producers, and do they change decisions?
- How do roads, energy and storage affect losses, costs and market participation?
- How do public budgets, grants, credit and insurance influence who can adopt a technology?
- How do buyer requirements shape production, quality, traceability and logistics?
- How do developments in mining, urbanisation or security affect agricultural land, labour, water or transport?

Record relationships only when supported. A proposed connection is a research question until evidence establishes that the relationship exists and how it works.

## Discovery and comparison sequence

1. **Orient broadly:** use the domains as a checklist across the state and its 17 LGAs.
2. **Identify relevant systems:** follow actual activities, locations and influence pathways rather than forcing every domain into every LGA.
3. **Compare coverage:** distinguish areas examined from areas not yet assessed; do not interpret missing information as absence.
4. **Trace relationships:** connect communities, activities, actors, institutions, research, rules, information, infrastructure, finance and markets where evidence supports the links.
5. **Investigate existing responses:** identify what is already done by public, private, community, academic and informal actors.
6. **Assess implications:** determine who is affected, what decisions or outcomes are influenced and whether a material need or opportunity exists.
7. **Deepen selectively:** pursue a focused investigation only when it could resolve a material uncertainty or change a decision.

## Documentation gate: keep GitHub factual and useful

The architecture and its topic lists may be documented as research principles without claiming that a particular organisation, programme, facility, relationship, problem or opportunity exists locally.

Do **not** use GitHub as a parking lot for unverified names, rumours, copied announcements, speculative opportunities or uncorroborated claims.

Before adding a factual discovery:
- Identify the exact claim being made and its geographic and time scope.
- Obtain a source that directly supports that claim; prefer authoritative primary records for institutional, legal, scientific and programme claims.
- Check the source date, issuing body, original context and whether the source is current enough for the claim.
- Distinguish what the source proves from what it merely reports, announces, proposes or claims.
- Seek corroboration where the claim is consequential, disputed, operational, commercial or likely to change.
- Do not convert a programme announcement into proof of implementation, a facility into proof of operating capacity, a membership list into proof of active reach, or a stated allocation into proof of disbursement.
- If the evidence does not meet the required standard, keep the item in the active research conversation or working notes for further checking; do not promote it into the repository as a factual finding.
- If an important issue remains unresolved, the repository may record the **question or topic to investigate**, without embedding the unverified claim as fact.

A research record should contain the claim, source and date, what the evidence establishes, its limits, and the reason it matters. Preserve earlier dated evidence when later evidence changes the picture; do not silently overwrite history.

## Repository structure and separation of concerns

This architecture is the conceptual map. It should not become a giant catalogue of every institution or topic.

Use separate records only when there is sufficient verified material and a clear reason to preserve it:
- **Community and geographic records:** verified location-specific findings and coverage.
- **Institution, research and programme records:** verified mandates, capabilities, activities and relationships.
- **Policy, law and standards records:** exact instruments, status, scope and authoritative references.
- **Activity and value-chain records:** evidence-backed production, processing and market systems.
- **Infrastructure and service records:** verified assets, operating status, capacity and access conditions.
- **Information and knowledge-network records:** verified sources, channels, coverage and use.
- **Opportunity and decision records:** evidence-backed needs, alternatives, buyer/user logic, constraints and decision rationale.

Link records instead of copying the same evidence into multiple places. Do not create a register merely because a category appears in this architecture; create one when there is enough verified content and a useful research or decision purpose.

## Boundaries

This architecture does not authorise outreach, contact, registration, payment, data collection from third parties, field activity, partnership representations, pilots or commitments. Obtain the user's explicit approval before any such action.
