# Research Principles

These principles govern how the project is researched, documented, compared and continued. They apply across all sectors, geographies, providers and research pathways.

## 1. Do not repeat completed work

Do not repeat or restart material already covered. Continue from the underlying research records and decisions. Revisit an earlier finding only when new evidence, a material change, a contradiction or a necessary validation justifies it. Record what changed and why.

## 2. Distinguish evidence from assumptions

Label independently verified facts, provider or organisation claims, third-party reporting, project hypotheses, estimates and unknowns. A website claim is not independent verification. A listed organisation is not a confirmed partner. A stated problem is not automatically validated demand.

## 3. Verify before recommending

Do not describe an opportunity as viable until its material requirements have been checked. Identify who can confirm each claim, the evidence needed and the missing response. Preserve uncertainty rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

## 4. Preserve the zero-capital constraint

Prioritise routes without upfront fees, compulsory purchases, inventory, equipment, paid licences or mandatory paid certification. Identify hidden costs, unpaid work, support obligations and other financial exposure. Defer opportunities that fail this filter unless the user explicitly changes the constraint.

## 5. Seek leverage through existing networks

Where relevant, investigate organisations and intermediaries that already possess trust, customer access, professional authority or distribution capacity. Do not equate follower counts or nominal membership with useful reach or activation. Network research is one tool within the wider discovery process, not the default starting point for every opportunity.

## 6. Require a value exchange

A proposed relationship must explain why the relevant provider, intermediary, customer or other participant would take part. Do not approach a network merely to ask it to share a referral link. Record responsibilities, incentives, costs, risks and permission.

## 7. Measure activation, not just reach

Distinguish nominal membership, active membership, communication access, decision-maker access, introductions, interest, agreed roles, demonstrations, qualified leads and actual adoption.

## 8. Keep distinct tracks distinct

Do not merge different products, buyer groups, use cases, geographies, operating models or partnership terms under one generic label. For Farmonaut, agriculture and mining remain separate assessment tracks unless evidence supports a specific connection. Preserve meaningful differences in all other research too.

## 9. Record negative findings

Keep rejected, deferred and unsuccessful leads with reasons and evidence. This prevents cycling back to unsuitable options without new evidence and makes the cost of prior exploration visible.

## 10. Maintain traceability

Record source URLs, source types, access dates, publication dates where available, evidence excerpts or summaries, confidence, verification status and what changed. Link structured workbook entries to detailed repository records. Do not detach claims from their supporting evidence.

## 11. No premature execution

Research and planning do not authorise outreach, paid commitments, registrations, implementation, software development or representation on behalf of another organisation. Obtain the user's explicit authorisation before external outreach or commitments.

## 12. Update structured and detailed records responsibly

After meaningful research, update the relevant detailed repository records and the companion spreadsheet's structured records when it is accessible. Preserve existing IDs and cross-references. If the spreadsheet cannot be accessed or updated, state that clearly; do not claim synchronisation. Keep repository evidence records authoritative for the supporting research.

## 13. Never depend on a single technology provider when alternatives exist

> Never allow a single technology provider to become the foundation of a market opportunity when credible alternatives exist.

Treat every provider as one candidate within a wider solution landscape. Before making a provider central to a market opportunity, identify and compare credible alternatives that address the same customer problem or a materially related part of the workflow.

Apply this principle as follows:

- Map direct competitors, functional substitutes, adjacent technologies and complementary providers; do not pretend they are identical products.
- Compare technical capability, evidence of performance, customer fit, geographic coverage, pricing and affordability, implementation requirements, support and known limitations.
- Separately compare partnership accessibility: individual eligibility, Nigerian eligibility, zero-capital entry, referral/reseller/channel terms, payout, lead attribution and permission to work through distribution networks.
- Do not select a provider solely because it contacted us first, has an attractive affiliate rate or has the most visible marketing.
- Preserve multiple viable options where possible. A provider may be preferred for one customer segment or use case and unsuitable for another.
- If only one credible provider is identified, document the search scope and why alternatives were not found; treat concentration as a known risk, not proof that the provider is best.
- Reassess the comparison when new evidence, product changes, service limitations, commercial changes or customer requirements arise.
- Never imply that multiple provider relationships are established merely because multiple providers have been researched.

The purpose is to reduce dependency, improve negotiating leverage, preserve customer choice and protect opportunities from a single provider's technical, commercial, geographic, operational or responsiveness limitations.

## 14. Explore broadly before committing narrowly

Do not allow the first interesting discovery to determine the entire research direction. Explore relevant locations, sectors, communities, organisations, economic activities, technologies and commercial models broadly enough to understand the available landscape before concentrating effort.

- Compare discoveries across relevant geographies and subject areas.
- Do not assume that the first community, LGA, sector, crop, organisation, provider or customer is the most important.
- Do not force findings into a preselected category such as a formal cooperative, a particular crop or a particular technology.
- Use discovery registers to make coverage and gaps visible; an unresearched entry is not evidence that nothing exists there.
- Broad-first is a default research discipline, not a reason to delay an urgent or unusually promising investigation. Record why an early deep dive is justified.

## 15. Discover before prescribing

Begin by understanding the environment, participants, activities, constraints, existing practices and actual problem. Do not begin every investigation by searching for a technology to distribute or a product to build.

A technology-first investigation is appropriate when the research question genuinely begins with a provider or technology, but it must still test the real problem, users, alternatives and market context.

## 16. Compare before prioritising

Do not let recency, familiarity, convenience or the order of discovery determine priority. Compare candidates using relevant evidence and explicit criteria. Mark missing evidence as unknown; do not convert unknowns into optimistic scores. Explain why a candidate is prioritised, deferred or rejected.

## 17. Preserve the parent mission across research pathways

A sector, geography, provider, organisation, pilot or case study is a pathway through which the mission is investigated, not the mission itself. Research pathways may change as evidence develops. Do not let an initial case study become a permanent boundary or centre without a deliberate, evidence-backed decision.

## 18. Existing solutions before building

Before proposing software, hardware or a new service, investigate established providers, existing platforms, startups, local operators and practical alternatives.

Prefer partnership, integration, implementation, distribution, referral, promotion or adoption of an existing solution when it can meet the need. Consider building only when:
- A genuine, material gap remains after examining existing solutions and emerging alternatives.
- The gap is supported by evidence rather than preference or technical curiosity.
- A concrete customer request, buyer incentive or other credible reason to build exists.
- Feasibility, cost, maintenance, operational responsibility and the user's constraints have been assessed.
- The user has approved moving beyond research.

Continue monitoring the solution landscape before committing to a build, because another provider may address the gap.

## 19. Deep dives must be justified

Detailed investigation, repeated verification, field research or pilot design should follow a clear reason: material uncertainty, credible opportunity, urgency, decision importance or new evidence. Define the question the deep dive must answer and the decision it could change. Do not expand research indefinitely without a decision purpose.

## 20. Research records, not snapshots, establish continuity

Do not rely on a manually maintained current-state or handover document as the authoritative account of the project. Such snapshots can become stale and conceal the underlying evidence.

- Continue from dated research records, decision records, source registers and the actual repository contents.
- Use the README as an index and orientation, the charter for purpose, principles for rules and changelog for repository edits.
- A changelog entry does not prove an external fact is still current.
- When status cannot be established from underlying records, mark it unknown and verify it.
- Update the source record when a finding changes; do not merely overwrite history with a new unsupported summary.

## 21. Keep the repository factual; do not dump unverified leads

GitHub is the durable record of the project's governing principles, research architecture, verified evidence and reasoned decisions. It is not a parking lot for every interesting name, rumour, copied announcement, search result or speculative opportunity encountered during exploration.

- It is acceptable to document **ecosystem principles, domain taxonomies, research topics, questions and methods** without claiming that a particular local entity or condition exists.
- Add a factual claim only when a source directly supports the exact claim, including its geographic and time scope. Use authoritative primary sources for laws, policies, institutional mandates, official programmes, scientific capabilities and public data wherever possible.
- For consequential claims about implementation, operating status, capacity, reach, funding, relationships, eligibility, commercial terms or demand, require appropriate corroboration. A single announcement or self-description rarely establishes these operational facts.
- Distinguish proposals, announcements, approvals, implementation, enforcement, disbursement and actual use. Never treat one stage as proof of another.
- Do not record an unverified organisation, programme, facility, relationship, problem or opportunity as an established local fact. Keep unresolved items in the active research process or temporary working notes until they meet the evidence standard.
- If continuity requires noting an unresolved matter in GitHub, record only the neutral **question to investigate** and why it matters; do not repeat the unsupported claim as fact.
- Every factual research record must state what the evidence establishes, what it does not establish, its source and date, and the relevant geographic and temporal limits.
- Prefer a smaller repository of reliable, traceable information over a large collection of weak leads.

This principle applies to every research pathway, including Plateau Agriculture Opportunity.

## 22. Resume from the exact recorded stopping point, regardless of the time away

A research session may pause for days, months or years. A pause does not reset the project, authorise a fresh start, or justify repeating completed work. The default is to continue from the last reliable state preserved in the repository.

At the beginning of every resumed session:

1. **Re-establish the project identity and scope.** Read the current repository structure, project charter, README/index files and governing research principles. Do not assume an old chat summary or remembered plan is more authoritative than the current repository.
2. **Locate the relevant research pathway.** Open its index and the underlying detailed records, decisions, source records and changelog entries relevant to the work being resumed.
3. **Reconstruct the stopping point from evidence.** Identify what was completed, what was deliberately deferred, what remains unresolved, the last actual research action and the next justified action. Use dated records and source references, not guesses.
4. **Check for overlap before doing anything.** Compare the proposed next action with existing records and prior coverage. Do not repeat a search, explanation, comparison, verification or documentation task merely because time has passed or the conversation has restarted.
5. **Check whether circumstances changed.** Revisit completed findings only when their time sensitivity, a new source, a material change, a contradiction, a decision need or a necessary validation justifies it. Make the reason for revisiting explicit and preserve the earlier dated record.
6. **State the continuation point briefly.** Tell the user what the records show was completed, what remains open and exactly where the work will resume. Do not make the user reconstruct project history that the repository can establish.
7. **Continue only after resolving material ambiguity.** If the records conflict, are incomplete or do not establish the stopping point, say precisely what is missing and investigate that gap. Do not invent continuity or silently restart from a convenient earlier stage.

The changelog records repository changes; it is not, by itself, a complete task tracker. The README is an index, not proof that its listed topics have been researched. The authoritative stopping point must be reconstructed from the actual relevant research records, decisions, evidence and dated work.

**No elapsed period—one week, one month, one year or ten years—automatically makes completed work new again.** The project resumes from its recorded state, while time-sensitive facts are revalidated only where necessary.

This rule applies project-wide to every sector, geography, research pathway, provider assessment and future workstream.
